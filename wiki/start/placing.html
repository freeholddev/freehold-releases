Placing a building

Pick a building from the build list on the charter screen, and you are in the same placement mode as with the charter — see Founding a settlement. Walk to move the model, scroll to turn it, right-click to place. Only a building whose cost can reach the settlement can be picked — see The charter screen.

What the model shows

The model shows how big the building is, not what it will look like: it is as high as the building will be over every block of its footprint, so roofs show as steps. The flat green or red rectangle under it is the ground that will be levelled, which is the wider figure of the two.

A pale yellowish post marks the front. It stands on the road connection, the spot where the road will meet the building, and a pale strip on the ground runs to it from the door. Where a building has a yard in front of its door, the strip lies under the model, so go by the post.

A village gate cannot be turned. It faces the settlement by itself, away from the village — see Village Gate.

Buildings of one kind do not all look alike. Every kind comes in more than one version — a different roof, a different entrance, different windows — and the game offers you one to start with, moving on to the next with every building you place. Sneak and scroll to look through the others; the name of the version stands above your hotbar. The footprint, the door and the goods port are the same in every version, so the choice never changes how a building is used, and a building keeps its version for good.

How a building goes up

  • A site levels the ground before it builds. The first stage flattens the whole footprint to one height, foundation included. Pick the spot with that in mind — the terrain you saw will not be there afterwards.
  • Holes are filled with the ground around them: sand in a desert, stone in rock, dirt on a meadow or in a forest.
  • A tree the footprint cuts into goes whole, trunk, branches and crown, and drops nothing. A tree that does not touch the footprint stays, even where its crown reaches over the building.
  • A chest, a bed, a sign or any other block that holds something keeps a building off its site. While such a block stands anywhere in the space the building will take, from the foundation up to the ridge, the model is red. Take the block away, then place.
  • Everything else on the site is cleared, and nothing drops. Earth, stone, plants, fences and whatever a player has built there go when the levelling reaches them.
  • A building rises block by block, at the pace your builders work. What you see is how far the work has really got. A wall that stops halfway up is waiting for the material of its next stage. More builders, or builders with hammers, and it grows faster.
  • A builder walks to the site first. The walk takes as long as a carrier's trip over the same way, and a built road makes it shorter.
  • Building work is counted in work units, and a builder with a hammer adds one a second. Without a hammer a builder adds a third of that; a builder who lives within reach of a tavern with beer adds a quarter more. Each building's page says how many units it takes and how many builders its site holds at once — a house of 600 units is ten minutes for one builder and a little over three for three, plus the walk there.
  • The material is paid in stages. The levelling comes first and costs nothing; then each stage takes its share of the material before its blocks go up, and the queue on the charter screen shows which stage a site has reached.
  • A building is built in the wood and stone of the land it stands on. The drawing says light wood, dark wood, rubble stone or masonry, and the biome at the building's place decides the block — see the table below. The model while you place shows none of this — it is the size, not the look.

A biome of another mod builds in the material of its kind of land — a modded pine forest in spruce, a modded desert in sandstone — as long as that mod files its biomes under the usual biome tags; Terralith's are found this way. Tearing a building down

Building blocks cannot be broken — not by strangers, not by you. The only way to remove a building is sneak + right-click on its anchor; that refunds half the material of the stages that were finished, as goods in the store. Nothing is left lying on the ground. The charter cannot be torn down at all. What fire, water or anybody else takes out of a building the settlement puts back by itself.

A block you set into an empty space of a building is yours to break again. Whoever may build in the settlement can put a chest, a lamp or anything else into a room and take it out later; only the blocks that belong to the building answer “This block belongs to X and cannot be broken.”

A torn-down building comes down from the top, layer by layer under a cloud of dust, and leaves the flat platform it stood on, topped with the ground around it — not the terrain that was there before it. The same holds for a building that burns down in a fight.

Room in front of the door

Around the spot where a road meets a building, a square stays free of every other building. That spot is the building's road connection, in front of its door, where carriers hand over. The square reaches 3 blocks to every side, and it holds both ways: a new building may not reach into the square of one that stands, nor have one standing in its own. Wall to wall is still allowed on every side without a door.

The reasons a road is refused are on Drawing a road.

Ground that some buildings need

Most buildings only want ground that is level enough. A few want something in particular, and their pages say what:

  • Quarry — rock under its pit
  • Fishing Hut — open water under its jetty
  • Windmill — an open stand
  • Mine and adit mine — ore in the rock
  • Opencast Quarry and Adit Mine — a hillside, see below

Building against a hillside

Two of the workings come in a second shape, for ground the first one refuses. The opencast quarry is the quarry, the adit mine is the mine — same goods, same speed, same tools, same cost. What differs is where they may stand and where they work: not under themselves, but into the slope behind them.

  • They need a rise behind them, and the preview says so. A place where the ground behind their working side is flat is refused: the slope behind the working side is too flat … Turn it so its working side faces uphill. Ten blocks behind the building the ground has to lie, on average across its width, at least three blocks above its floor — one notch in the hill does not spoil a good place.
  • An adit mine also needs ground under its whole drift. The drift runs level into the hill for six blocks. A dip, water, lava or open air in its way refuses the place, with the same message as for a flat slope.
  • If it refuses, turn it before you walk on. The working side is the back of the building. A spot at the foot of a hill is refused when the building faces the hill with its front; one turn and the same spot is green.
  • They may be set into the hill, not only at its foot. Towards the working side the ground over the site may rise up to six blocks above the floor; the site cuts it away while it levels. From side to side the ground may vary by only two blocks, as for other buildings.
