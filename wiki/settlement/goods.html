Goods and carriers

The store

The store is a weight, not an inventory. The charter gives it room for 2 400 slots and every storehouse 1 200 more; planks weigh 3 each, raw wood 6, bread 1, a tool 2 — every weight is on All goods. There is no chest to open, and nothing comes back out — you can give, not take.

Handing goods in

The goods port takes basic goods only, and the settlement makes everything else itself. Right-click the goods port of the charter or a storehouse with a stack in hand, or let a hopper feed it. Basic are:

  • the tools axe, fishing rod, saw, hammer and hoe;
  • the food bread, fish (cod and salmon) and fruit (apples);
  • planks and logs of every kind of wood.

Anybody may hand goods in, unless the server says otherwise. A visitor or a player without a role may give the settlement goods at its goods ports; a server can keep that to the freeholder and the members.

Anything else — the pickaxe and shovel, rubble, ashlar, fittings, iron, fuel, torches, beer, emeralds, a soldier's gear — is refused with “The settlement makes X for itself; only basic goods are handed in here.”, and a hopper simply keeps it. A settlement can still build everything: every building of level 1 costs only planks or goods the settlement's own buildings make. Saplings are never taken: foresters and orchards plant without them.

A server can open the store to every good; then the goods port takes everything that has an item. Both settings are on Game rules.

Nothing comes out of the store, not even into a hopper. What lies in the store leaves it only through the settlement: by building, by being used up, by trade, or carried off by raiders.

Goods do not teleport

Every building has two piles of its own: what has been delivered to it, and what it has made and nobody has fetched yet. It works out of its own pile and puts what it makes on its own pile — no building reaches into the store from across the settlement.

What moves goods between the piles and the store is a carrier, and there is always at least one: the first resident of any settlement becomes one, before the builder and before any work place. That is the one thing worth knowing in advance, because it means a settlement needs one resident more than it has posts. With the four you start with that works out exactly: a carrier, a builder, a feller and a sawyer. The forester is the one that has to wait for a fifth resident, and a house is what lets one move in.

Goods go the short way, and that is not always through the store. If a building makes something another one uses, and the two are close enough to each other, a carrier takes the load straight across instead of walking it into the store and out again. The settlement works it out per trip and takes whichever is shorter — so a hut standing next to its sawmill feeds it directly even when the store is nearer to both. Nothing about it is announced; you see it by watching where the carrier walks.

A carrier moves a load, not the whole order. One trip carries 12 slots of storage weight — two logs, four planks, twelve loaves — and whatever does not fit follows on a later trip. Most deliveries to a house fit in one; the planks for a building stage and a pile that has been left standing do not. A cart triples that. Your toolmaker builds one from six planks and two fittings, a carrier without one takes it out of the store the next time it sets off, and from then on it moves 36. A cart wears out after 120 trips; a carrier who stops carrying leaves it in the store. The toolmaker keeps a spare for every carrier and two more, so once your smithy makes fittings, carts look after themselves.

And a carrier brings something back rather than two walking the same way. When one is already on its way to a building, nothing else is sent there in the meantime: the one arriving picks up whatever is waiting and carries it off on its way home. Building your producers near the places that use them is therefore worth twice — the load goes direct, and the carrier comes home loaded.

Distance matters, and so does the ground. How long a trip takes comes from the way between the two ends: its length, every block it climbs or drops, and water or a scarp a walker has to go round. A building on the flat a stone's throw from the store is served in a few seconds; the same building across a ravine takes many times that. So where you put a building is a decision about supply — and a road makes delivery faster rather than possible. Carriers walk round finished buildings, so a settlement gets a little slower as it builds itself up, which is what a road answers.

A building is reached only if it has a way to a store: within 256 blocks of the charter or a storehouse, through a building within that range it trades goods with, or along a finished road. Without any of them it says “X cannot be reached: there is no way to it from anywhere in the settlement.” and will not start. The way out is a road from it to a building that is reached, or a storehouse within range. Inside the charter's own claim this cannot happen; it matters at the edge of land that watchtowers have grown far out.

A site and the finished building need different goods, so a building can be cut off although it could be built: a house's site is reached through a sawmill near it, but the finished house needs bread and fuel. It says so as soon as it stands.

Four consequences you will see:

  • A building keeps working while a carrier is walking. That is what the piles are for. A trip is not a standstill.
  • Far-flung buildings are the ones that starve first. With one carrier and four buildings, the far one waits longest — the carrier is walking, not idle. More residents mean more carriers.
  • A busy carrier pays every distance twice. Its next errand starts where the last one ended, so serving two buildings on opposite sides of the settlement costs the way back in between. Building close together is worth as much as building close to the store.
  • A full store stops the fetching, and the fetching stops the work. Nobody can unload into a store with no room, so the pile at the building fills up, and then the job stops. The message stands at the building where you can see it, and the settlement says the store is the reason.

A storehouse is a second door, not a second pile — it adds room, and a trip that runs over a store runs over the nearer one. See Storehouse.

Targets and thresholds

Each building keeps a target for what it consumes and a threshold for what it makes: it orders up to the target and is fetched from once it is over the threshold. Both are yours to change on the buildings tab of the charter screen — never per building: a target for every building of one kind and level, a threshold for every building of one kind. See The charter screen.

Raising a target buys a buffer, and pays for it in walking. A sawmill that wants twenty raw wood instead of twelve keeps sawing longer while its carrier is away, but the wood is out of the store the whole time and cannot go anywhere else. Lowering a threshold fetches earlier and walks more often, which is worth doing where the walk is short and wasteful where it is long.

Who gets a scarce good

Between buildings of one kind, a scarce good goes to the one furthest short of its target. Two sawmills waiting for raw wood are served by who lacks more.

Between kinds, the share decides. Some goods are used by two or more kinds of building, or by a workshop and your building sites, listed below. Each of these kinds has a share of the good, in per cent, and the shares of one good always add up to 100.

The building sites count as one kind. Planks, fittings and rubble are used by sites and by workshops alike; every site, upgrade and road in the queue together are one kind, Building sites. So a long queue does not stop the toolmaker. Among themselves the sites go by the order of the build queue.

A hearth counts as a kind like a workshop. The fuel for the hearths of your houses is shared out with the bakery, brewery, smelter, smithy and toolmaker. So when fuel is short, the houses get their share and no more — and from level 2 a house with a cold hearth takes nobody in, see The fire in a building.

Only so much is made

  • Every trade stops when the settlement has enough. The rule is not only about tools, see the toolmaker: a settlement wants a bounded amount of anything, and above it nothing is made. The sawmill stops when there are enough planks, the lumberjack when there is enough raw wood, and each of them says "has stopped: the settlement has enough" and names the good. What "enough" is grows with the settlement, because it counts who uses the good — every sawmill wants raw wood in front of it, the toolmaker wants planks, and every builder's place counts for the material it builds with, so a builders' yard raises the plank stock the settlement keeps ready. Spend the good and the work starts again on its own: lay out one building and the sawmill goes back to sawing.
  • Trade posts raise the ceiling by their sale targets, so the settlement goes on making a good to have a surplus to sell — see Selling.
  • The ceiling bounds what is made, never what you hand in yourself. If you want a great pile of planks in reserve, build another storehouse and hand them in.
