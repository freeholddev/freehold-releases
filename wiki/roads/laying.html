Once the route is finished

The route goes into the same build queue your buildings stand in, at the end. From there it is an ordinary building site with two differences: an earth road costs no material, and every one of its stretches levels — that is the work.

Buildings come first, unless you say otherwise. As the settlement starts out, your builders go to a road only while no building site can be worked on. A growing settlement waits for material most of the time, so the road comes on in those gaps, a stretch at a time. If you want roads sooner, turn down Buildings before roads on the politics tab of the charter screen.

The road is laid a stretch at a time, sixteen columns to a stretch, from the start towards the end. Each column is dug down or filled up to the height the drawing showed you, and a path block goes on top. A tree in the way goes whole, as on a building site, and drops nothing.

What you lay is an earth road. Wood and stone come later, by themselves, on a road that carries enough traffic and meets the higher grade at one of its ends — see Road grades.

Every road you finish counts. It shortens the way between its two ends for every trip that passes that way, whether or not goods run between exactly those two buildings. A road between two lumberjack huts is therefore not wasted: it is a piece of the network. A road never makes a trip longer, and its blocks are protected like a building's from the moment they are laid.

Crossing lanterns

A crossing is marked by a lantern beside the paving. You cannot break it and it costs you nothing — it appears with the crossing and goes with it. It shows where one stretch of road ends and the next begins, which is what you need to know before you tear anything up. A crossing right at a building's door has no lantern, but is a crossing all the same.

The lantern shows the grade of the crossing, and that is the grade of its highest road, and it changes by itself when that grade changes:

  • Earth road: a wooden post with a torch on top.
  • Wooden road: a wooden gallows with a lantern hanging over the road.
  • Stone road: a gallows on a post of stone bricks, with iron fittings.

What a road forbids

A road is its surface and the two blocks of air over it. You cannot break the path blocks, and you cannot set anything down in the space above them either — not a chest, not a fence, not a torch on the ground. Blocks that were already standing may be taken away as usual. Your foresters plant beside a road, never on it. And should grass grow back over a column, the settlement lays it anew by itself — see What the settlement repairs.

And you cannot put a building on top of one. A site whose footprint would cover a road is refused while you are still placing it. Move the site a few paces, or tear the road up first.

Walking on it

Carriers walk the quickest way through the network. Where a finished road is quicker, the carrier walks the road; where open ground is quicker, or there is no road, it walks straight across and goes on along the next road from there. Half road, half meadow is therefore an ordinary sight.

A road is about twice as fast as open ground, and faster with every grade. Thirty blocks of level open ground take 18 seconds, thirty columns of earth road 9. So a road pays even where it goes well round: on the flat an earth road is still taken when it is twice as long as the straight line, a stone road up to three times.

A road that runs past a building takes it in by itself. Nobody has to reach a road at its end or at a crossing: a carrier walks from the door to the nearest point of a road within 32 blocks and goes on along it, if that is quicker than the straight way. There is nothing to build for it. A spur from a building to a road nearby is therefore not needed for the carriers to use that road.

Two roads that do not meet are joined across a short gap. Where an end or a crossing of one road lies within 32 blocks of another road's, a carrier walks the one road, crosses the gap on foot and goes on along the other. The gap is no road: a trip across it counts towards neither road's traffic, and a trade post still trades only with a village it reaches over finished roads.

Workers and builders use the roads too. A worker going between home and work takes them unless that is much of a detour; a builder on the way to a building site takes whatever way is quickest. A lumberjack going to a tree or a farmer to the field walks straight there.

Water is swum, and it costs time. Settlers prefer any dry way round; where they have to swim, a block of water takes as long as two blocks of walking. They cannot drown. If you want a dry crossing, build a road over it — that is what the causeway is for.
